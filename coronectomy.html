Coronectomy

Taking out part of a lower wisdom tooth

All leaflets

You have been offered a coronectomy. This leaflet explains what it is, why we have offered it, your other choices and the risks.

Please read it before your appointment. Write down any questions and bring them with you.

What is a coronectomy?

A tooth has two parts. The crown is the part you can see. The roots hold the tooth in your jaw bone.

In a coronectomy, we take out the crown of your wisdom tooth. We leave the roots in your jaw. Your gum heals over them.

Why does my wisdom tooth need treatment?

A wisdom tooth that is stuck can cause problems, such as:

  • gum infection that keeps coming back
  • decay (holes) in the wisdom tooth or the tooth in front of it
  • a cyst, which is a sac of fluid in the jaw (this is rare)

Why not take out the whole tooth?

A nerve runs through your lower jaw. It gives feeling to your lower lip and chin.

Your X-rays show that the roots of your wisdom tooth are very close to this nerve. Some people also need a 3D scan, called a CBCT, to show this clearly.

If we take out the whole tooth, we could damage the nerve. This can leave your lip and chin numb or tingly. For a few people, this lasts for life.

If we leave the roots where they are, we do not disturb the nerve. This makes nerve damage much less likely.

What are my choices?

  1. Leave the tooth alone. Your dentist can keep an eye on it. If it keeps causing infections or pain, these problems may carry on or get worse.
  2. Take out the whole tooth. This gets rid of the tooth, but the risk to the nerve is higher.
  3. Coronectomy. We take out the crown and leave the roots. The risk to the nerve is much lower.

Your surgeon will talk through which choice suits you best. The choice is yours. You can change your mind at any time, even after you have signed the consent form.

A coronectomy is not right for all people. It is not safe if the tooth is decayed, or if there is infection around the roots. Your surgeon will check this.

Before your appointment

  • Tell us about all the medicines you take. This means ones from your doctor and ones you buy.
  • Tell us if you take a blood thinner, such as warfarin, apixaban or rivaroxaban.
  • Tell us if you take, or have ever taken, a medicine for your bones, such as alendronic acid or denosumab.
  • Keep taking your medicines unless we tell you to stop.
  • If you are having sedation or a general anaesthetic, you will need an adult to take you home. Your letter will tell you if you need to stop eating and drinking.

What happens on the day?

You can have the operation in one of three ways. Your surgeon will talk to you about which is best.

  • Awake, with an injection to numb the area (local anaesthetic).
  • Awake but relaxed, with a numbing injection and medicine through a vein (sedation).
  • Asleep, with a general anaesthetic. Most people go home the same day.
  1. We open the gum and remove a little bone around the tooth.
  2. We cut off the crown of the tooth and take it out.
  3. We smooth the roots so they sit just below the bone.
  4. We close the gum with stitches. These melt away by themselves in about 2 weeks.

It takes about 20 to 40 minutes. You should not feel pain. You may feel pushing. You may hear the drill.

After the operation

  • Your mouth will be sore for a few days. Take painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, if they are safe for you. Do what the packet says.
  • Your face may swell and bruise. It is often worst 2 to 3 days after the operation. It goes down over 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Your jaw may feel stiff, so it may be hard to open wide. This gets better as the swelling goes down.
  • Most people need a few days off work or school. Do not play sport or do heavy work for a few days.
  • After sedation, do not drive for 24 hours. After a general anaesthetic, do not drive for 48 hours.

Looking after your mouth

  • Do not rinse your mouth on the day of the operation. Rinsing can start the bleeding again.
  • For the first day, hold an ice pack wrapped in a towel against your cheek, on and off, to help the swelling.
  • From the next day, rinse gently with warm salt water after meals, for about a week. Use 1 teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm water.
  • Keep brushing your teeth. Be gentle near the wound.
  • Eat soft food for a few days. Do not have hot drinks until the numbness has worn off.
  • Try not to smoke for at least the first few days. Smoking slows healing and makes infection more likely.
  • If it bleeds, roll up a clean, damp handkerchief or piece of gauze. Bite on it firmly for at least 20 minutes without checking.

When should I get help?

Phone us if:

  • bleeding has not stopped after you have pressed on it for 20 minutes, twice
  • pain or swelling gets worse after the third day
  • you feel hot and unwell, or there is a bad taste or smell from the wound
  • your lip, chin or tongue is still numb the day after the operation

Go to A&E or call 999 if:

  • you find it hard to breathe or swallow
  • swelling spreads to your eye or down your neck
  • bleeding is heavy and will not stop

Will I need to come back?

Not everyone needs another appointment. If you do, we may take an X-ray to check the roots.

If the area causes problems later, even years later, contact us or your dentist.
