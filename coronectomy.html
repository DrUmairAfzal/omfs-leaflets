In short

  • We take out the crown (the top part) of your wisdom tooth. We leave the roots, because they are close to a nerve.
  • This makes nerve damage much less likely than taking out the whole tooth.
  • Expect pain and swelling for a few days. Most people go home the same day.
  • About 3 to 5 in 100 people need a second operation later to take the roots out.

You have been offered a coronectomy. This leaflet explains what it is, why we have offered it, your other choices and the risks.

Please read it before your appointment. Write down any questions and bring them with you.

What is a coronectomy?

A tooth has two parts. The crown is the part you can see. The roots hold the tooth in your jaw bone.

In a coronectomy, we take out the crown of your wisdom tooth. We leave the roots in your jaw. Your gum heals over them.

Why does my wisdom tooth need treatment?

A wisdom tooth that is stuck can cause problems, such as:

  • gum infections that keep coming back
  • decay (holes) in the wisdom tooth or the tooth in front of it
  • a cyst, which is a bag of fluid in the jaw (this is rare)

Why not take out the whole tooth?

A runs through your lower jaw. It gives feeling to your lower lip and chin.

Your X-rays show that the roots of your wisdom tooth are very close to this nerve. Some people also need a 3D scan of the jaw (a CBCT) to check this.

Taking out the whole tooth could damage the nerve. This can leave your lip and chin numb or tingly. For a few people, this lasts for life.

If we leave the roots where they are, we do not disturb the nerve. This makes nerve damage much less likely.

What are my choices?

  1. Leave the tooth alone. Your dentist can keep an eye on it. If it keeps causing infections or pain, these may carry on or get worse.
  2. Take out the whole tooth. This gets rid of the tooth, but the risk to the nerve is higher.
  3. Coronectomy. We take out the crown and leave the roots. The risk to the nerve is much lower.

Your surgeon will talk through which choice suits you best. The choice is yours. You can change your mind at any time, even after you have signed the form to say yes.

A coronectomy is not right for everyone. It is not safe if the tooth has decay or the roots are infected. Your surgeon will check this.

Before your appointment

  • Tell us about all the medicines you take. This includes ones you buy from a shop.
  • Tell us if you take a medicine to thin your blood, such as warfarin, apixaban or rivaroxaban.
  • Tell us if you take a medicine for your bones, such as alendronic acid or denosumab. Tell us even if you have stopped it.
  • Keep taking your medicines unless we tell you to stop.
  • If you are having or a , arrange for an adult to take you home. Your letter will tell you if you need to stop eating and drinking.
  • Plan a few days off work or school.
  • Have some soft food at home.
  • Have paracetamol or ibuprofen at home, if they are safe for you.
  • Have an ice pack, or a bag of frozen peas, ready to wrap in a towel.

What happens on the day?

There are three ways to have the operation. Your surgeon will talk to you about which is best.

  • Awake, with a numbing injection. This is called a .
  • Awake but relaxed. You have a numbing injection, and medicine into a vein to make you calm and sleepy. This is called sedation.
  • Asleep. Medicine puts you fully to sleep. This is called a general anaesthetic. Most people still go home the same day.
  1. We open the gum and remove a little bone around the tooth.
  2. We cut off the crown of the tooth and take it out.
  3. We smooth the roots so they sit just below the bone.
  4. We close the gum with . These melt away by themselves in about 2 weeks.

It takes about 20 to 40 minutes. You should not feel pain. You may feel pushing, and you may hear the drill.

After the operation

  1. On the day
    • Your mouth is numb for a few hours.
    • Bite on gauze if it bleeds.
    • Do not rinse your mouth.
  2. Days 2 to 3
    • Swelling and bruising are often at their worst.
    • Your jaw may feel stiff.
  3. Up to 2 weeks
    • The swelling goes down.
    • The stitches melt away.
  • Your mouth will be sore for a few days. Take paracetamol or ibuprofen, if they are safe for you. Follow the instructions on the packet.
  • Your jaw may be stiff, so it may be hard to open wide. This gets better as the swelling goes down.
  • Most people need a few days off work or school. Do not play sport or do heavy work for a few days.
  • After sedation, do not drive for 24 hours. After a general anaesthetic, do not drive for 48 hours.

Looking after your mouth

  • On the day, do not rinse your mouth. Rinsing can start the bleeding again.
  • On the first day, hold an ice pack wrapped in a towel on your cheek, on and off. This helps the swelling.
  • From the next day, rinse gently with warm salt water after meals, for about a week. Use 1 teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm water.
  • Keep brushing your teeth. Be gentle near the wound.
  • Eat soft food for a few days. Wait until the numbness has gone before you have hot drinks.
  • Try not to smoke for at least a few days. Smoking slows healing and makes infection more likely.
  • If it bleeds, roll up a clean, damp cloth or piece of gauze. Bite on it firmly for 20 minutes. Do not keep checking it.

What are the risks?

Every operation has some risks. These numbers come from studies of thousands of patients. Your surgeon will talk to you about your own risks. '5 in 100' means 5 out of every 100 people who have this operation.

Pain, swelling, bruising and a stiff jaw for a few daysMost people
The roots move a little in the first year, usually upwardsCommonThis is normal. It does not often cause problems.
The roots come loose during the operation, so we take them out tooAbout 5 in 100If this happens, the risk to the nerve is the same as taking out the whole tooth.
A second operation later to take the roots out3 to 5 in 100Usually because the roots come up through the gum or get infected. This can be months or years later.
Infection2 to 4 in 100
Dry socket: the area becomes very painful and is slow to healAbout 1 in 100
Numb or tingly lower lip and chinLess than 1 in 100
Numb or tingly tongue, or a change in tasteLess than 1 in 100

Nerve damage most often gets better, but this can take up to 18 months. For a few people, a small patch stays numb for life.

When should I get help?

☎Phone us if:

  • bleeding does not stop after biting on gauze for 20 minutes, twice
  • pain or swelling gets worse after the third day
  • you feel hot and unwell, or the wound tastes or smells bad
  • your lip, chin or tongue is still numb the day after the operation

!Go to A&E or call 999 if:

  • you find it hard to breathe or swallow
  • swelling spreads to your eye or down your neck
  • bleeding is heavy and will not stop

Will I need to come back?

Not everyone needs another appointment. If you do, we may take an X-ray to check the roots.

If the area causes problems later, even years later, contact us or your dentist.
