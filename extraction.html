In short

  • Most teeth come out in one piece. Some need a small operation with stitches.
  • Bite on gauze and do not rinse on the first day, so a blood clot can form.
  • From the next day, rinse gently with warm salt water.
  • Phone us if the pain gets worse 2 to 4 days later. This may be a dry socket.

You have been offered treatment to take out a tooth. This leaflet explains what happens, your choices, the risks, and how to look after your mouth afterwards.

Please read it before your appointment. Write down any questions and bring them with you.

What happens when a tooth is taken out?

A tooth sits in a hole in the jaw bone, called a . Most teeth can be eased out of the socket in one piece.

Some teeth need a small operation. This might be because the tooth is broken, buried, or has curved roots. We open the gum, and may remove a little bone or cut the tooth into pieces. We then close the gum with .

Afterwards, a blood clot forms in the socket. This is the start of healing. Over the next few weeks, gum and new bone fill the space.

What are my choices?

  1. Keep the tooth. Some teeth can be saved with a filling, or a crown. Ask your dentist if this is possible for you.
  2. Leave things as they are. If the tooth is infected or painful, this may get worse. Infection from a tooth can spread.
  3. Take the tooth out. This is what this leaflet is about.

Think about whether you want to fill the gap. You could have a denture (false tooth), a bridge or an implant. Your dentist can talk these through with you.

The choice is yours. You can change your mind at any time, even after you have signed the form to say yes.

Before your appointment

  • Tell us about all the medicines you take. This includes ones you buy from a shop.
  • Tell us if you take a medicine to thin your blood, such as warfarin, apixaban or rivaroxaban.
  • Tell us if you take a medicine for your bones, such as alendronic acid or denosumab. Tell us even if you have stopped it.
  • Keep taking your medicines unless we tell you to stop.
  • If you are having a numbing injection, eat a light meal a couple of hours before.
  • If you are having or a , you will need an adult to take you home. Your letter will tell you if you need to stop eating and drinking.
  • Have paracetamol or ibuprofen at home, if they are safe for you.

What happens on the day?

  1. We numb the area with an injection. This can make your heart beat faster for a few minutes. This is normal.
  2. We loosen the tooth and take it out. You should not feel pain, but you will feel pushing and pressure.
  3. If you need stitches, they melt away by themselves in about 2 weeks.
  4. You bite on a piece of gauze to help a clot form. We check the bleeding has stopped before you leave.

Some people have sedation or a general anaesthetic instead. Sedation is medicine to make you calm and sleepy. A general anaesthetic puts you fully to sleep. Your surgeon will talk to you about this if it suits you.

After your tooth is taken out

  1. On the day
    • Bite on gauze.
    • Do not rinse your mouth.
    • Your mouth is numb for a few hours.
  2. The next few days
    • Your mouth is sore, then gets better each day.
  3. The next few weeks
    • Gum and new bone fill the socket.
  • Until the numbness wears off, take care not to bite your lip, cheek or tongue.
  • Your mouth will be sore for a few days. Take paracetamol or ibuprofen, if they are safe for you. Follow the instructions on the packet.
  • Most people can go back to work or school the next day.
  • Do not drink alcohol or do hard exercise for 24 hours.

Looking after your mouth

  • On the day, do not rinse your mouth. Rinsing can start the bleeding again.
  • From the next day, rinse gently with warm salt water after meals, for about a week. Use 1 teaspoon of salt in a cup of warm water.
  • Keep brushing your teeth. Be gentle near the wound.
  • Eat soft food for a few days. Wait until the numbness has gone before you have hot drinks.
  • Try not to smoke for at least a few days. Smoking slows healing and makes infection more likely.
  • If it bleeds, roll up a clean, damp cloth or piece of gauze. Bite on it firmly for 20 minutes. Do not keep checking it.

What are the risks?

Your risks depend on which tooth is coming out. Your surgeon will talk to you about your own risks. This list follows your consent form. Numbers come from national guidance or studies. Common means more than 1 in 20 people, Less common fewer than 1 in 20 and Rare fewer than 1 in 100.

During or just after the operation

Top back teeth only: a small hole into the sinus (the air space above the roots)CommonWe usually spot this straight away and close it. There is a separate leaflet about this.
BleedingLess commonBiting on gauze usually stops it.
Damage to your lips, gums or the tooth next to itLess common
The tooth breaksRareWe may leave a very small piece of root if taking it out would do more harm than good.

Broken jawRare

When should I get help?

☎Phone us if:

  • bleeding does not stop after biting on gauze for 20 minutes, twice
  • the pain gets worse instead of better, often 2 to 4 days later. This may be a
  • you feel hot and unwell, or the wound tastes or smells bad
  • top back tooth only: drinks come out of your nose, or air passes between your mouth and nose

!Go to A&E or call 999 if:

  • you find it hard to breathe or swallow
  • swelling spreads to your eye or down your neck
  • bleeding is heavy and will not stop
